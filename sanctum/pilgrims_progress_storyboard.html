sanctum_pilgrim_wake.davar

The control surface, as actually built (sanctum_avatar.h)

Not 18 sliders. Two authored things exist and disagree with each other: a 23-control registry (3 domains, 12 slots, 9 material channels) written for a fuller creator, and the base body mesh, which only actually exposes 6 real macro axes — gender, age, muscle, weight, height, proportion — built from 17 raw MakeHuman morph targets combined in the products of those six axes. The file's own words: "the two sides disagree in both directions." The registry asks for face detail (nose width, jaw width, ear projection) the base has no target for at all; the base's two strongest axes — gender and age — have no control in the registry. A coverage check (sav_coverage()) reports the mismatch rather than hiding it, so a control that can't move the mesh is never drawn as if it can.

  • Skin noise was a GPU driver defect, not a texture bug. A gradient-free skin sample stayed noisy unless it was the only statement in a ~500-line shader. Workaround: a texture-free skin material routed through the engine's existing subsurface-scattering skin model instead. Measured per-patch noise: ~15.4 → ~7.4.
  • Gender and age were silently undrivable for a while. A default that suppressed ethnicity-specific morph targets (reasoned as keeping blends "inside the universal family") turned out to delete the only three targets in the file that carry any gender or age shape at all — their universal counterparts measure zero displacement. Fixed by deriving the suppression from how many distinct ethnicities the base actually ships, rather than defaulting it off.

The zone

Not a ring of pedestals — a full walked zone. A ziggurat ("the house on the height"), a temple/colonnade, three mixed groves (oak, pine, olive) plus a landmark tree and a bare dead tree, a lion (its behaviour driven by a real simulated connectome of 512 Drosophila neurons — idle/seek/flee are the fly's own states, repurposed as an animal AI), and at the threshold a Hebrew door-word (pethach) beside a relic bearing the divine name.

Thirteen callings, scattered, not arranged

Each stands near a real landmark — a grove, the architecture, the threshold — because a calling names you alone and its near() disc must not overlap another's. Three keep the positions they were originally authored at (Adherent, Steward, Loresmith); the other ten were placed by a solver, close enough to a landmark to be legible, far enough apart that no two discs fire as one.   • Two of the thirteen Hebrew titles were caught wrong by a whole-text search, not by ear. Almoner's title had been 'almon (H489, "bereavement / widowhood") — an English sound-alike of "almon-er," not a translation — replaced with dayyan ("judge/advocate"), the person-noun standing in the calling's own anchor verse. The root reasoning was right, the specific word was never attested. Corrected to mizmor, attested 57 times, all in Psalm superscriptions — the word actually in Psalm 33:2, this calling's own anchor. A repeatable gate (verify_calling_hebrew.py) now checks all 13 titles against both codices; this was the only one of 13 that failed on first run.
  • Nine of the thirteen scripture anchors moved on 2026-08-06 when Ryan ruled "the manual wins" over this file's older set — and CallingsData.luau's own header names the risk directly: the ruling had only been applied in sanctum_grove.davar, so this file would have silently reverted it the next time anything read from here instead of there.

The campus

Six buildings around a central ascending temple: Scripture Hall, Prayer Chapel, Skill Forge, Questorium, the World Gateway, and the Archives. Verified against both real sources directly: TutorialData.luau's own onboarding text says "the five main buildings: Scripture Hall, Prayer Chapel, Skill Forge, Questorium, and World Gateway" — that sentence is real, word for word, and it really does only name five. AcademyZones.luau's actual building array builds six, Archives included, at the exact positions this scene scales ×3 (Scripture Hall (−70,0,0), Prayer Chapel (70,0,0), Skill Forge (0,0,−70), Questorium (0,0,70), World Gateway (−90,0,30), Archives (90,0,30)). The scene follows the array, not the prose. The temple at the centre is explicitly Ezekiel/Revelation-inspired, golden doors, deliberately built taller than every other building on campus because the manual's own word for it is "ascending."

None of that is in sanctum_academy.davar yet; the shipped scene is npcs() plus one govern() on the archive.

"Let the garden be read out of the record."

What's read from the record vs. authored here

Every other zone in this lane names its own trees and landmarks by hand. This one doesn't: world("eden") pulls Eden's real biome, flora, and landmark names live from the game's own 15-world source record. The file is explicit about where that stops: the record gives names ("Tree of Life Grove"), never positions, so the layout — where each named thing actually stands — is authored in this scene, not pretended to come from the record too. Likewise species() is a mapping choice, not a translation: the engine only grows four tree rules (oak, pine, olive, dead) against 37 distinct flora terms across all 15 worlds, falling back to oak for anything it can't match.

  • This scene was the first hard stop in the whole player path. An old line tried to place() the flood water as if it were a placeable form; lake() actually carves its basin at its own coordinates and returns a count, not a form. The mismatched call errored on every single run, which aborted every binding after it, which meant close() could never fire — so the chain simply stopped here, four scenes past the calling grove, for as long as it took someone to walk welcome-through-Noah end to end and watch exactly where the flow died.
  • The road to here was edited twice to stop skipping scripture. next() first ran straight from Noah to Mamre (Genesis 9 → 18), missing Abram's birth and calling. A scene for Ur was inserted to cover that (Genesis 11:31) — then Babel was inserted again, ahead of Ur, because the direct Noah→Ur hop still skipped Genesis 10–11:9, where the nations divide and the tower goes up: "a player who has not stood on the plain of Shinar does not feel Ur."
  • Seven figures were authored for "noah_ark" in the game's NPC data, and a direct count confirms exactly seven — unlike the Academy's mismatched figure, this one checks out precisely.
  • A separate, much deeper layer exists for this chapter that the native engine never reads: the Roblox quest-density lane's Chapter 52 pass added 141 quests and 705 activities to "Noah Ark / Mount Ararat," with the dove and rainbow tied to specific fenced days of a 370-day flood timeline (day 604976 onward). None of that density reaches this scene, which tells the same story in one room with one choice.

Hospitality at Mamre

The first scene in this document framed explicitly as a written quest being played, not a hand-authored scene. The distinction matters and the file states its own limit plainly.

What this scene admits about the whole quest system

The game's quest data holds 547 arcs and 3,142 quests. This engine's own zone-closing loop — a scene closes once every near() has fired — already happens to match three of that data's objective types: meet_npcs (386 quests), visit_zones (110), and gather (32). This scene runs one of those 386 for real. The other roughly 2,600 quests ask for theology, worship, scripture_memory, survive_days — mechanics this engine doesn't have. objective() reports those as UNJUDGED rather than silently passing them: "a quest system that reports success for a mechanic it never implemented is worse than one that admits the gap."

"Let one of the written quests be walked."
opening speak()

A genuine translation call, not a default

Genesis 13:18 / 18:1's Hebrew, 'elonei mamre, is rendered "oaks" in some translations and "terebinths" in others — a real, old ambiguity. The scene grows terebinth, not oak: botanically, both candidate species (Quercus calliprinos and Pistacia palaestina) actually co-dominate this exact Judean maquis, and terebinth's broad-low-dome habit fits a goat-browsed pastoral hillside better than a temperate oak's round canopy. Scaled down deliberately too — the real tree reaches 5–18 m but is "often only 1–3 m where heavily browsed by goats," which is Abraham's actual landscape.

What the city atlas actually is, and isn't

83 cities, each with ten real research dimensions of prose — setting, population, water engineering, trade, religion, law, health, networks, archaeological significance. It's research, not geometry: no coordinates, no building list. So this zone speaks Jericho truly from that prose and builds a generic walled city, because the atlas can't supply the second and pretending otherwise would be an overclaim the rest of this lane already refuses to make elsewhere.

The fight that got put in twice, and taken out twice

This scene ran trial("guardian", 5) — a timing ring whose own failure state is literally collapse, against a wall that falls on the seventh circuit — for about an hour before a real measurement made a stronger case: across every wired scene, the chain held sixteen skill checks and zero fights, while 49 authored enemies and an entire unused combat system sat idle. Sixteen of the same verb in a row needed the floor to change. Jericho's own authored enemy, canaanite_giant, seemed like the obvious place.

It was tried, and reverted, twice, for two different real reasons:

  • Measured, not guessed: a genuinely fresh character walked from the welcome screen arrives here at level 27 (455 hp, 45 strength). canaanite_giant is 3000 hp / 144 damage — death in 3.2 hits against 67 needed to win. The engine scales enemies with the player, so no amount of levelling ever closes that gap. "Both were executions, not encounters."
  • The real reason: Joshua 6 has no battle in it. The city isn't taken by the sword — seven circuits, seven trumpets, a shout on the seventh day, and the wall falls down flat. Swapping in combat "for variety" was a game-design reflex that made the engine assert a siege the text explicitly denies — the same rule already written into a different scene about Daniel and the lions, three hours earlier, and broken here without noticing.

Restored to the timing ring. Real combat still exists where the story actually has peril — a separate consequence branch off the Babylon scene — variety earned by the text, not imposed on it.

Babylon

Psalm 137, Daniel, Ezekiel — the first real branch in the whole game
"By the rivers of Babylon, we sat down and wept."
opening speak()

Where the Bābili fix actually came from

This is the scene that first discovered Babylon wasn't in the atlas at all — the original 83-city atlas simply didn't have it, so city("Babylon") returned "no city in the atlas." A larger, real 238-city CDLI-built atlas existed already, but only inside a separate DNCZ store, and got installed only after a set comparison proved it a strict superset of the old one: 238 vs 83, zero keys lost, 155 gained. Then a second, worse bug turned up underneath the first: even after installing it, only 128 of 238 cities ever loaded, because a hardcoded cap silently dropped the other 110 every single load. The drop counter's own comment said "REPORTED, never silent" — and nothing in the code had ever actually read it. Cap raised, counter wired to print.

A second silent hard stop, twin of Noah's

The willows of Psalm 137:2 were bound with near(river, ...) — and like lake(), river() carves terrain and returns a number, not a form. The line errored on every run, which killed everything after it — the lion's near() and the close() both — so Babylon never handed off to the next scene. Same class of bug as Noah's flood water, caught the same way: by walking the whole chain and watching exactly where it stopped. The scripture itself was kept in the file the entire time it was broken, commented out and waiting, because "the words are the point of the scene" — restored once the willows had a real placed form (a cluster of poplars) to hang the near() on instead of the river directly.

The first real branch

Ryan, in-thread: "For Daniel it wasn't a fight but if that character betrayed the king perhaps it would be?" This scene is that question, built. Two governs stand here and they do not lead to the same place:

govern(lion)"You keep the window open toward Jerusalem, three times a day, where they can see you." → continues on to Nehemiah, as before
govern(decree)"You let the window stay shut for thirty days, and there is nothing for anyone to report." → branches instead to sanctum_lions_den.davar

The wording is flagged as a first draft, Ryan's to correct: Daniel 6 is theologically delicate, since the man who defies the decree is the one who's delivered — so "betrayal" can't simply mean praying. The safe path is written thin on purpose rather than invent a theology for it. The branch itself is enforced by the engine: a form can only gate a path once it actually has a govern() to check, so the branching line has to come after both choices are declared, not before.

Combat considered here too, and refused for the same reason as Jericho

babylonian_soldier exists as a real authored enemy, and a lions' den is the obvious place a designer reaches for combat. Refused anyway: this scene's own near() already quotes Daniel 6:22 — "My God sent his angel and shut the lions' mouths." The whole point of the chapter is that it wasn't a fight; arming one would have the engine contradict the verse printed three lines above it.

Real named people, not a generated pool

Ryan: "use names based on city for each type of merchant kind/lord etc." The alternative already existed elsewhere in the source tree and was literally "Merchant 1" through "Merchant 589." Here, every dweller is a person the excavated tablets actually attest at Babylon — found by the scribal determinative mark written before a name in the cuneiform itself ({disz} for a man, {f} for a woman) and given a role by the kind of document that names them. Babylon's tablets attest 54 lords, 14 stewards, 13 townsfolk, 5 merchants, 4 messengers — among the stewards, Egibi, the actual historical banking house this city is remembered for. Both this and the craft data below refuse to run at all without city() called first: a person and a skill both belong to a place.

What the atlas shows, and what it honestly doesn't

Babylon's atlas record carries ten dimensions and only five have real content; the other five are literal enrichment placeholders ("Requires dedicated trade-route and commodity enrichment," and so on). Those five are deliberately never shown to a player — "showing him the scaffolding and calling it the building." Of what does show: population_social reports the actual genres and languages the excavated tablets carry — astronomical, royal/monumental, legal; Akkadian, Sumerian, Elamite — astronomical leading at 41% of the whole record, "the corpus agreeing, on its own, that Babylon is the sky-watching city." Nine real crafts stand here from a 108,897-record cuneiform index.

Nippur

Ezekiel 1–37 — the first scene built by a genuinely different method
"The canal called Chebar. The captives are settled here, and the heavens are opened."
opening speak()

The first two-pass scene

A generator derived everything below that comes straight out of the record — the atlas description, the crafts Nippur's own tablets attest, its named dwellers, the dated events — and then refused to write five lines on its own: what the ground says, the decision it asks, the word it ends on, the two spoken lines. The scene was held out of the game as a hard failure until those five were replaced from a real source, not invented. Every quoted line below was read out of a 31,102-verse Bible database, not written from memory.

A real bug the generator caught in itself

It first reported "0 dated events at Nippur" — which was false. The chronology doesn't key events under the city's name at all; it keys them under "Chebar Canal, Nippur region" and "Tel-abib" separately, two different namespaces than the city lookup uses. Trusting that zero would have meant authoring a generic Mesopotamian city on top of the exact ground where Ezekiel saw the heavens open. The generator now says so explicitly instead of reporting a bare zero.

The archaeology and the book agree, and nobody arranged it

Nippur's excavated tablets come out 44.4% administrative, 17.7% literary, 14.3% lexical — a city of clerks and copyists, storehouse reckoning and sign-lists. Ezekiel is a priest given visions and told to write them down, in the one place in the ancient world whose entire trade was writing things down so they'd survive the people who wrote them. The file notes this is the same pattern Susa shows later (61.4% administrative tablets under a book about irrevocable royal decrees) — "it keeps happening because the record is real."

Where he actually sat

Ezekiel 3:15 — called "the quietest verse in the book" — has him sit down where the captives sit, for seven days, before he prophesies anything at all. The chronology files him at Tel-abib across four separate dated events spanning eleven years: not a stop on the way to the visions, but where he lived.

What the ratio actually says

2,225 tablets were excavated at Nimrud, Kalhu's site. 57.8% (1,287 of them) are royal or monumental — a king's deeds cut into stone. Prayer and incantation together are 3.6%; omens are 3.0%. "This city wrote down its rulers sixteen times more often than it wrote down its prayers." Of the 315 named people the tablets attest here, 304 are lords; the other eleven — a steward and ten merchants — were never carved into anything. The govern() below is built directly on that asymmetry.
